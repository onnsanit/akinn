The system

One piece, not two

The mark is a single silhouette built from two circles. Where they overlap, both patterns stay visible and neither one wins. That overlap is the whole argument, and it is the only part of the mark you cannot pull back apart.

The name is the argument

Akin means related. Six colours come off one wheel and are dealt alternately to the two halves, and one band — the echo — is drawn with the same motif in the same colour on both sides. The kinship is structural, not a mood: you can point at it.

Everyone gets a different one

Each mark is generated from a six‑character seed. No visitor gets the same pattern twice; every visitor gets the same family. Share the seed and you share the mark — akinn.com/?seed= .

On the reference. The concept — ornament from traditions and centuries that could never have met, forced into one form until you notice they are related — is Nevin Aladağ's, from her Pattern Kinship series. We saw it as Pattern Kinship, Bubbles, ping-pong (2022, acrylic on waterjet-cut aluminium) at the Harn Museum of Art.

On what is ours. Nothing here is traced. Every motif is drawn from scratch by the generator out of the shared grammar of ornament — chevron, rosette, lattice, meander, scallop, ogee, bar, dot — forms that recur independently across almost every decorative tradition, which is exactly why the kinship reading holds. The palette, the geometry and the reduction are ours. If this direction goes forward, credit the reference publicly and do not reproduce her colourways.
